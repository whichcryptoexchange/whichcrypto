Malta is one of the few EU states that had a dedicated crypto licensing regime before MiCA, and it has attracted some of the best-known international exchanges. It is also the subject of a published ESMA peer review of how it authorised a crypto firm. This guide covers both.

*Note: the MFSA's website could not be reviewed for this article, so national forms and fees aren't covered here. The application requirements below are MiCA's common rules, which apply in every member state.*

## Background

The MFSA describes Malta as "an early regulatory adopter with respect to crypto assets back in 2018 when it introduced the Virtual Financial Assets (VFA) framework."[^peer] Under MiCA, Malta kept the full 18-month transitional period for existing providers.[^grandfathering] That period ended on 1 July 2026 at the latest.[^art143]

## Applying

The MFSA applies MiCA's rules like any other authority. You need an EU registered office, effective management in the EU and at least one EU-resident director, plus the full Article 62 application file and €50,000 to €150,000 of minimum capital depending on your services. The regulator has 25 working days to check the file is complete and 40 working days to decide.[^art63] See [MiCA authorisation explained](/learn/mica-casp-authorisation-explained/) for the full list.

## What ESMA's peer review found

In April 2025, "following a series of events", ESMA's Board of Supervisors launched a fast-track peer review of the MFSA's authorisation and early supervision of one crypto-asset service provider.[^peer] ESMA published the executive summary on 10 July 2025, with the firm's name redacted.

ESMA rated the MFSA in three areas:[^peer]

| Area | ESMA's assessment |
|---|---|
| Supervisory settings and resources | Fully meeting expectations |
| The authorisation process | Partially meeting expectations |
| Supervisory review and use of powers | Largely meeting expectations |

On the authorisation, the review committee "questions the timing of the authorisation of the specific entity under MiCA given that several material issues remained unresolved at the time of the authorisation." It said the process "should have been more thorough", and that it "did not find evidence that certain key aspects of the authorisation file were adequately assessed", including governance, conflicts of interest, ICT and custody risks, and certain anti-money-laundering controls.[^peer]

It also noted strengths. The MFSA "has built a good level of expertise in this sector and has sufficient supervisory resources", and ESMA praised its recruitment, training and early outreach to industry.[^peer]

The MFSA said it "respects the views of the PRC and accepts the findings of the report", and that it and Malta's financial intelligence unit "are onboarding the recommendations".[^peer]

## What this means if you're choosing Malta

A peer review of one authorisation is not a verdict on every Maltese licence. But it has two practical consequences. First, ESMA's recommendations went to every national authority, asking them to look harder at business-plan growth, conflicts of interest, group arrangements, ICT and DeFi exposure before authorising.[^peer] Expect that scrutiny wherever you apply. Second, banks, partners and counterparties who do due diligence on you can read the same report. Be ready to explain how your own authorisation was assessed.
