People choosing where to get a crypto licence often hear that some jurisdictions are "premium" and others are where firms go for less scrutiny. Those labels are opinions. This article sets out what regulators and EU bodies have actually published, so you can judge for yourself. We don't characterise any individual firm.

## Why the home regulator matters under MiCA

A MiCA authorisation from any member state lets a firm serve the whole EU. ESMA notes that the most common model is "a single entity hub in Europe providing crypto services, through a passport, to the entire EU single market, while the main client base is not necessarily in the home NCA."[^peer]

That is the source of the concern. If one authority is less thorough, its authorisations still reach every member state. ESMA says this is why "CASP authorisation is of paramount importance to ensure that investors in any jurisdiction across the EU will be given the adequate level of protection, regardless of the jurisdiction of the origin of the CASP".[^peer] In December 2024 ESMA's Board agreed that key authorisation cases would be discussed between authorities before authorisation was granted.[^peer]

## Evidence 1: who cut the transition short

MiCA let existing firms carry on under national rules until 1 July 2026, but allowed member states to shorten or skip that period "where they consider that their national regulatory framework applicable before 30 December 2024 is less strict than this Regulation."[^art143] ESMA published each state's choice:[^grandfathering]

| Transitional period | Member states |
|---|---|
| 6 months | Finland, Hungary, Latvia, Netherlands, Poland, Slovenia |
| 9 months | Sweden |
| 12 months | Austria, Germany, Ireland, Lithuania, Norway, Slovakia |
| 18 months (maximum) | Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, France, Greece, Iceland, Italy, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Malta, Portugal, Romania, Spain |

Read this carefully. A shorter period means a state used a power whose stated basis is that its old national rules were less strict than MiCA. That is a statement about the old regime, not about how strictly the state authorises under MiCA today. Some 18-month states also set early application deadlines to qualify.[^grandfathering]

## Evidence 2: ESMA's peer review of Malta

In 2025 ESMA carried out a fast-track peer review of how the Malta Financial Services Authority authorised and supervised one crypto firm. It found the MFSA "fully meeting expectations" on resources, "largely meeting" on supervision, and "partially meeting expectations" on the authorisation process.[^peer] The committee questioned "the timing of the authorisation of the specific entity under MiCA given that several material issues remained unresolved".[^peer] The MFSA accepted the findings.[^peer] More detail is in our [Malta guide](/learn/crypto-licence-malta/).

This is one review of one authorisation. It shouldn't be read as a ranking of all regulators. ESMA lists its peer reviews publicly.[^peer-list]

## Evidence 3: published approval rates

Few regulators publish outcomes. The UK FCA does. Of 395 crypto registration applications decided since January 2020, 17% were registered and 67% withdrawn.[^fca] That is a hard number for how demanding a gateway is. If a regulator you are considering doesn't publish one, ask advisers for their direct experience.

## Evidence 4: licences that didn't survive the transition

Estonia's Financial Intelligence Unit issued crypto licences until the end of 2024. When it stopped, 43 remained valid.[^fiu] All of them lost validity on 1 July 2026.[^fiu-vasp] In March 2026 Finantsinspektsioon was processing 10 MiCA applications.[^fi] Our register shows {{home_count:EE}} Estonian MiCA home-state entries today. A company offered for sale with an old Estonian licence does not come with a usable authorisation.

## Evidence 5: enforcement on the record

Some regulators publish enforcement actions. Dubai's VARA lists its actions publicly, most of them against unlicensed activity.[^vara] After the MiCA transition ended, ESMA told unauthorised providers to "immediately stop onboarding new EU clients", and said authorities "may, where necessary, take coordinated action against unauthorised CASPs".[^wind]

## How to use this

None of these measures on its own makes a regulator "premium" or "light-touch". Together they tell you what counterparties can check: how the jurisdiction handled the transition, whether its regulator has been peer-reviewed, how often it says no, and who else chose it. Your banking partners, payment providers and institutional clients can read the same documents.
