The EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) replaced separate national crypto regimes with one authorisation that works across the whole single market. This guide covers the rules every applicant faces, whichever country it chooses. National regulators add their own forms and fees, which the country guides cover.

## Who needs authorisation

Article 59 is the gatekeeper. A person may not provide crypto-asset services in the EU unless it is either "a legal person or other undertaking that has been authorised as crypto-asset service provider in accordance with Article 63", or an existing credit institution, central securities depository, investment firm, market operator, e-money institution, UCITS management company or AIFM allowed to provide crypto services under Article 60.[^art59]

That second route matters when you read the registers. Banks and investment firms don't need a new licence. They notify their regulator, and they appear on ESMA's register alongside fully authorised crypto firms.

Article 59 also sets the substance rules. An authorised firm must have its registered office in a member state where it carries out at least part of its services, "their place of effective management in the Union and at least one of the directors shall be resident in the Union."[^art59] A letterbox entity run from outside the EU does not qualify.

## What the application must contain

You apply to "the competent authority of their home Member State".[^art62] Article 62 lists what the file must include, among them:[^art62]

- a programme of operations setting out the crypto-asset services you will provide
- proof that you meet the prudential safeguards in Article 67
- your governance arrangements
- proof that the management body is "of sufficiently good repute" and has the knowledge, skills and experience to run the firm
- the identity of shareholders with qualifying holdings
- internal control mechanisms, including for money-laundering and terrorist-financing risk, and a business continuity plan
- technical documentation of your ICT systems and security arrangements
- how clients' crypto-assets and funds will be segregated
- complaints-handling procedures
- where relevant, your custody policy, trading platform rules, execution policy and how you will provide transfer services

The detailed content is set by a regulatory technical standard, and the application form by an implementing regulation. Germany's regulator, for example, tells applicants to use "das Antragsformular aus dem Anhang der Verordnung" (the application form annexed to the regulation).[^bafin]

## How long it takes

Article 63 puts the authority on a clock:[^art63]

| Step | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Acknowledge receipt | 5 working days |
| Assess whether the application is complete | 25 working days from receipt |
| Decide on a complete application | 40 working days from receipt of the complete application |
| Notify the decision | 5 working days after deciding |
| Pause to request more information | once, up to 20 working days |

If your file is incomplete, the authority sets a deadline to fix it and can refuse to review it after that.[^art63] In practice the clock only starts when the regulator accepts your file as complete, so the realistic timeline depends on how well-prepared the file is.

## Minimum capital

Article 67 requires prudential safeguards of "at least the higher of" the permanent minimum capital in Annex IV and "one quarter of the fixed overheads of the preceding year, reviewed annually."[^art67] Annex IV sets three classes:[^annex4]

| Class | Minimum | Services |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | €50,000 | executing or receiving and transmitting orders, placing, transfer services, advice, portfolio management |
| 2 | €125,000 | class 1 plus custody, or exchanging crypto-assets for funds or for other crypto-assets |
| 3 | €150,000 | class 2 plus operating a trading platform |

## Passporting

Once authorised, a firm may "provide crypto-asset services throughout the Union".[^art59] To do so cross-border, it tells its home authority which member states it will serve, which services and from when. The home authority passes this on to the host states within 10 working days, and the firm may start "at the latest from the 15th calendar day after having submitted the information".[^art65]

This is why most of the market runs on passports. On our register, {{home_count:DE}} entries have their home in Germany, but every country page shows far more firms reaching that country by passport than authorised there directly.

## The transitional period is over

Article 143(3) let existing providers keep operating "until 1 July 2026 or until they are granted or refused an authorisation", and let member states shorten that period.[^art143] Many did: ESMA's list shows periods from 6 to 18 months.[^grandfathering]

On 23 June 2026 ESMA told firms that had not been authorised to "immediately stop onboarding new EU clients" and wind down their EU activities in an orderly way.[^wind] It also reminded firms outside the EU that they "cannot provide MiCA services to EU clients or solicit EU clients", adding that "this also applies in a business-to-business context."[^wind]

## Choosing a home member state

MiCA is meant to be applied the same way everywhere, but your home authority does the assessing and the supervising. ESMA has said the most common business model is "a single entity hub in Europe providing crypto services, through a passport, to the entire EU single market".[^peer] That makes the choice of home regulator one of the biggest decisions you make.
